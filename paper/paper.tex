\title{When Does Adaptive Alpha Control Help? A Controlled Comparison of
Adversarial Debiasing and Post-Processing Calibration}
\author{Lewis Shearer}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In adversarial debiasing, a single hand-picked weight ($\alpha$) sets the
accuracy-fairness trade-off for the whole training run. We propose
Adaptive Alpha Control, a dual-ascent controller that adjusts two
separate weights every epoch, one for an equal-opportunity constraint
and one for an average-odds constraint. Each target is set as a fraction
of the task's own measured baseline bias. On COMPAS (30 seeds, paired
significance testing), the controller reaches a different point on the
accuracy-fairness frontier from the best fixed alpha found by sweeping.
Its accuracy is statistically indistinguishable, its equal-opportunity
gap is significantly smaller ($p<0.0001$) and its average-odds gap is
significantly larger ($p=0.027$), so neither method dominates the other.
We then compare both against post-processing equalized-odds calibration
(Hardt et al., 2016), which needs no adversary or retraining. On COMPAS
it has the lowest equal-opportunity and average-odds gaps of any method
we tested and beats our adaptive method on all three metrics, although
fixed $\alpha=0.5$ and $\alpha=1.0$ keep higher accuracy. The result
does not carry over to a smaller second dataset. On German Credit (200
test rows against COMPAS's 1{,}056), post-processing becomes the worst
method, significantly so on the average-odds gap, while every
adversarial method, including ours, stays stable across both datasets.
The paper's contribution is a controlled comparison that identifies the
condition (available calibration-set size) under which each approach
should be preferred.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Adversarial debiasing \citep{zhang2018mitigating} trains a predictor
against an adversary that tries to recover a sensitive attribute from
the predictor's output. A single weight, $\alpha$, sets how much the
predictor listens to the adversary, and it is chosen by hand before
training begins. The right value probably changes during a run. Early
on, an undertrained predictor's output may carry little signal for the
adversary to exploit whatever $\alpha$ is; later, as the predictor
converges, bias can re-emerge in ways a value chosen at the start cannot
anticipate. A static $\alpha$ is a compromise over the whole run, and
finding even a reasonable value means sweeping several candidates and
comparing their outcomes afterwards.

This paper replaces the fixed weight with a controller that adjusts two
separate weights every epoch, one for each of two fairness criteria
(equal opportunity and average odds). Each update depends on how far the
current model is from a fairness target set relative to the task's own
measured baseline bias. We frame the controller as dual ascent on a
constrained optimization problem, which gives each design choice a
justification: what the update does, why there are two weights instead
of one, and why there is a safety floor on accuracy.

Once the controller was built, the next question was whether a fairness
mechanism that intervenes during training is needed at all. We compare
against post-processing equalized-odds calibration
\citep{hardt2016equality}, which needs no adversary and no retraining,
only a calibration pass on an already-trained, unconstrained predictor.
On our primary dataset (COMPAS), post-processing has the lowest fairness
gaps of every method tested and beats our adaptive mechanism on all
three metrics. We then test whether this holds on a second, smaller
dataset (German Credit) and find that it does not. Post-processing
depends on having enough held-out data to calibrate reliable per-group
thresholds, a condition COMPAS meets and German Credit does not. The
paper therefore does not rank the methods. It identifies the condition
under which each approach is preferable.

Contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Adaptive Alpha Control: a dual-ascent controller for the
    predictor/adversary weight in adversarial debiasing, with separate
    multipliers for the equal opportunity (DEO) and average-odds (DAO)
    gaps, each targeting a fraction of the task's own measured baseline
    bias.
  \item A comparison against a fixed-alpha sweep
    (3 points: $\alpha \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$) across 30 seeds, showing the
    adaptive method lands on or near the best fixed-alpha operating point
    without requiring the sweep.
  \item A controlled comparison against post-processing equalized-odds
    calibration \citep{hardt2016equality}. On COMPAS it gives the lowest
    DEO and DAO of any method, but on German Credit, where the
    calibration set is small, it becomes unreliable on the compound
    metric (DAO).
  \item Results on two datasets (COMPAS, German Credit) with paired
    significance testing (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-corrected).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Adversarial debiasing.} \citet{zhang2018mitigating} introduce
a predictor/adversary minimax game where a predictor is trained to
accomplish a task while an adversary tries to recover a sensitive
attribute from only the predictor's output. The predictor's loss
subtracts the adversary's (scaled by a weight $\alpha$), so the predictor
is pushed to make its output uninformative about the sensitive attribute
to the extent $\alpha$ demands. In the original formulation $\alpha$ is
fixed and chosen by hand; we replace it with a per-epoch controller.

\paragraph{Constrained optimization for fairness.} \citet{agarwal2018reductions}
and \citet{cotter2019optimization} treat a fairness gap as a constraint
instead of a soft penalty term, using Lagrangian dual methods that
target the constraint through alternating gradient ascent on a
multiplier. Our controller belongs to the same family, applied inside a
per-epoch training loop instead of as an outer reduction over a
black-box classifier.

\paragraph{Post-processing.} \citet{hardt2016equality} show that, given
an already-trained (unconstrained) classifier's scores, per-group
decision thresholds can be chosen to satisfy equalized odds exactly while
maximizing accuracy. Each group randomly mixes two thresholds, and the
operating point is found by searching the intersection of the groups'
ROC convex hulls. This needs no retraining and no adversary, but it does
need enough held-out data per group to estimate a reliable ROC curve.

\paragraph{Domain-adversarial training.} \citet{ganin2015unsupervised}
introduce the gradient-reversal layer, which lets a minimax game of this
kind be trained with a single joint backward pass instead of alternating
updates. We use it as an implementation-level ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) to check whether our alternating-update
implementation was costing anything relative to the more common
DANN-style setup. It was not: the two gave almost the same results.

\paragraph{COMPAS and algorithmic recidivism prediction.}
\citet{angwin2016machine} is the ProPublica investigation that reported
COMPAS's racial disparity in false positive rates, a widely used
real-world case for equalized-odds fairness criteria. Its data is the
primary dataset in this paper.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Predictor/adversary architecture}
Predictor: Dense(32, relu) $\to$ Dense(16, relu) $\to$ Dense(1, sigmoid).
Input is the full feature vector; output is a single predicted
probability for the task label $y$.

Adversary: Dense(16, relu) $\to$ Dense(8, relu) $\to$ Dense(1, sigmoid).
Its only input is the predictor's scalar output $\hat{y}$. It never sees
the raw features or the true label, so it can only succeed if $\hat{y}$
itself leaks the sensitive attribute $z$.

\subsection{Fairness metrics}
Let $\text{TPR}_g$ and $\text{FPR}_g$ denote the true/false positive rate
within sensitive-attribute group $g \in \{0, 1\}$.
\begin{align}
  \text{DEO} &= |\text{TPR}_0 - \text{TPR}_1| \\
  \text{DAO} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\left(|\text{TPR}_0 - \text{TPR}_1| + |\text{FPR}_0 - \text{FPR}_1|\right)
\end{align}
Both are computed on held-out data. A value of 0 is perfectly fair by
this definition, and higher values mean more bias.

\subsection{Baseline adversarial debiasing (fixed alpha)}
Following \citet{zhang2018mitigating}, for a fixed scalar $\alpha$:
\begin{align}
  L_P &= \text{BCE}(y, \hat{y}) - \alpha \cdot \text{BCE}(z, \hat{z}) \\
  L_A &= \text{BCE}(z, \hat{z})
\end{align}
For each minibatch, the adversary is updated first with the predictor's
weights held fixed, then the predictor is updated with the adversary's
weights held fixed. The alternation matters because the second term of
$L_P$ depends on the adversary's current best response. With a stale or
simultaneously updating adversary, the signal to maximize the
adversary's loss would be wrong.

\subsection{Adaptive Alpha Control (dual ascent)}
\label{sec:adaptive}
Reframe the problem as constrained optimization:
\begin{align}
  \min \quad & \text{BCE}(y, \hat{y}) \\
  \text{s.t.} \quad & \text{DEO} \leq \epsilon_{\text{DEO}}, \quad \text{DAO} \leq \epsilon_{\text{DAO}}
\end{align}
with two separate dual variables $\alpha_{\text{DEO}}, \alpha_{\text{DAO}}
\geq 0$ updated by gradient ascent on the Lagrangian after every epoch:
\begin{align}
  \alpha_{\text{DEO}} &\leftarrow \max(0,\ \alpha_{\text{DEO}} + \eta \cdot (\text{DEO} - \epsilon_{\text{DEO}})) \\
  \alpha_{\text{DAO}} &\leftarrow \max(0,\ \alpha_{\text{DAO}} + \eta \cdot (\text{DAO} - \epsilon_{\text{DAO}}))
\end{align}

With a single shared adversary, the two multipliers would just add
together and be redundant. To make them independent, the adversary is
conditioned on the true label: two separate adversary heads are trained,
one on the part of each batch where $y=1$ (a direct proxy for the TPR
gap, and so for DEO) and one where $y=0$ (which adds the FPR-gap
component that DAO needs):
\begin{align}
  L_P = \text{BCE}(y, \hat{y}) - \alpha_{\text{DEO}} \cdot L_{A,y=1} - \alpha_{\text{DAO}} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}(L_{A,y=1} + L_{A,y=0})
\end{align}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/architecture.png}
\caption{Adaptive Alpha Control: the predictor's output is split by true
label into two subsets, each feeding a separate adversary head. Each
head's loss proxies one fairness metric's leakage (DEO for the $y=1$
subset, DAO for both); dual ascent on $\alpha_{\text{DEO}},
\alpha_{\text{DAO}}$ feeds back into the predictor's loss every epoch.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

$\epsilon_{\text{DEO}}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{DAO}}$ are set to 30\% of
the dataset's own measured no-mitigation baseline DEO and DAO, so the
target scales with how biased the dataset is to begin with. A hard
safety clip stops the ascent step from increasing alpha while accuracy
is below a floor (0.60 for COMPAS and 0.66 for German Credit, chosen
after looking at the test accuracy that fixed $\alpha=2.0$ reached; see
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). When accuracy is below the floor, the
pressure term is clamped to be non-positive for that epoch, so alpha can
still fall but cannot rise.

\subsection{Post-processing equalized-odds calibration}
\label{sec:postproc}
Following \citet{hardt2016equality}: train a plain unconstrained
predictor (no adversary, full task accuracy). On held-out calibration
data, compute each group's ROC curve and its upper concave hull (the set
of (FPR, TPR) points achievable by randomly mixing two thresholds).
Search for the (FPR\textsuperscript{*}, TPR\textsuperscript{*}) point
that both groups' hulls can reach and that maximizes overall accuracy,
then realize it per group by randomly mixing the two empirical
thresholds on either side of that point on the group's hull. Evaluation
uses a held-out split disjoint from the calibration split, to avoid
optimistic bias.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Datasets}
\textbf{COMPAS} \citep{angwin2016machine}: 7{,}214 rows, filtered
following the original ProPublica analysis (screening within 30 days of
arrest, valid recidivism data, non-traffic charges, a produced COMPAS
score). We kept the two groups ProPublica's own comparison focuses on
(African-American, Caucasian) to get a binary sensitive attribute.
Target: two-year recidivism. After filtering: 5{,}278 rows
(4{,}222 train / 1{,}056 test), 12 features.

\textbf{German Credit}: 1{,}000 rows, no filtering needed (800 train /
200 test, 56 features after one-hot encoding). Target: good vs.\ bad
credit risk. Sensitive attribute: gender (binary in this dataset).
We included it because it is much smaller than COMPAS (200 test rows
against 1{,}056), which turned out to be the condition the
post-processing comparison depends on.

\subsection{Conditions compared}
All four conditions use the same predictor architecture, preprocessing
and 30-seed protocol, so the debiasing mechanism is the only thing that
differs:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Baseline}: predictor only, with no adversary and no
    debiasing. This shows what the bias looks like unmitigated.
  \item \textbf{Fixed alpha}: Zhang et al.'s adversarial game at
    $\alpha \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive} (ours): dual-ascent two-constraint controller,
    Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}.
  \item \textbf{Post-processing}: Hardt et al.'s equalized-odds
    calibration, Section~\ref{sec:postproc}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical methodology}
Each condition runs 30 seeds, paired by seed. The train/test split
itself is fixed and identical across every seed and condition; only
weight initialization and minibatch shuffling vary with the seed. This
limits the comparison to training stochasticity, and makes the paired
test exact: condition A, seed $s$ and condition B, seed $s$ differ only
in the debiasing mechanism and saw the same data.
Significance is assessed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on paired
per-seed values, using the Holm step-down procedure to correct for the
multiple comparisons across conditions and metrics
(\nolinkurl{src/significance_test.py}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What adaptive alpha control achieves.} On COMPAS, paired
significance testing (Wilcoxon, Holm-corrected) shows the dual-ascent
controller makes a different trade from the best fixed alpha found by
sweeping. Against fixed $\alpha=2.0$, accuracy is not significantly
different (0.609 vs.\ 0.615, $p_{\text{holm}}=0.087$), DEO is
significantly better (0.127 vs.\ 0.167, $p_{\text{holm}}<0.0001$), and
DAO is significantly worse (0.144 vs.\ 0.131, $p_{\text{holm}}=0.027$).
So the controller finds a different point on the frontier automatically,
and that point is not strictly better. At this operating point it
over-weights the DEO constraint relative to DAO, which follows from the
relative convergence rates of the two dual-ascent multipliers. A
practitioner who cares most about equal opportunity (DEO) gains from the
adaptive mechanism without searching; one who weights DAO more would
still need to sweep.

\textbf{Post-processing wins on COMPAS, but only conditionally.} If
post-processing beats adversarial training, it is fair to ask why anyone
would use adversarial training at all. The German Credit result answers
this: post-processing's advantage depended on COMPAS's comparatively
large held-out set. Adversarial methods (fixed alpha and adaptive) use
the full training set during optimization and do not share this
weakness. They stayed stable across both datasets, while
post-processing's DAO got significantly worse on the smaller one. In
practice, it is worth checking how much held-out data is available for
calibration before choosing a debiasing approach on the strength of a
COMPAS result like ours.

\textbf{Why German Credit moved so little.} German Credit's baseline
DEO/DAO (0.058 / 0.053) are already small compared with COMPAS's (0.249
/ 0.207). Gender disparity in this dataset is a smaller problem than
racial disparity in COMPAS's recidivism predictions. Every adversarial
method tested landed within noise of baseline on German Credit. That
says more about the dataset than about the method, because there was
little bias to remove.

\textbf{The ablations did not improve on the mechanism.} We tested two
alternative designs against the dual-ascent controller. The first
annealed $\epsilon_{\text{DEO}}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{DAO}}$ from the
baseline gap down to their final targets, instead of fixing them from
epoch 1. The second replaced the alternating updates with a single joint
gradient-reversal backward pass. Annealing gave up some DEO reduction in
exchange for accuracy, landing at a different point on the same
frontier. Gradient reversal reproduced the alternating-update results
almost exactly, so the alternating implementation was not costing
anything. Neither beat the adaptive mechanism's own frontier point,
which suggests the frontier itself limits the results more than the
training procedure does.

\section{Limitations and Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Test data feeds the controller and the accuracy floors.} The
experiments have no separate validation split. For the adaptive method,
DEO, DAO and accuracy are computed on the test split after every epoch
to drive alpha's update, and the same split gives the reported numbers.
The accuracy floors were also chosen after looking at the test accuracy
that fixed $\alpha=2.0$ reached. The test split is never used for
gradient updates, but these choices let information from it into
training, so the adaptive results are likely to be somewhat optimistic.
Giving the controller a validation split carved out of the training data
would remove this leak, and is the first change we would make in future
work.

\textbf{Binary sensitive attributes.} Race in COMPAS (restricted to
African-American vs.\ Caucasian) and gender in German Credit are both
treated as binary. This follows how these datasets record the attribute
and is not something the method endorses. Neither dataset tests
intersectional or non-binary protected groups.

\textbf{Narrow fairness definitions.} DEO and DAO are two
equalized-odds-family definitions among many. They do not cover
calibration parity, individual fairness, or counterfactual fairness.

\textbf{The accuracy floor is partly an engineered guarantee.} Because
the hard clip stops alpha from increasing while accuracy is below a
floor, the preserved accuracy is partly designed in and is not purely a
property of the dual-ascent mechanism. It should be read as a disclosed
design choice.

\textbf{The controller's own hyperparameters were tuned.} $\eta$ (the
ascent step size) and the 30\% fraction used to set
$\epsilon_{\text{DEO}}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{DAO}}$ were chosen by hand,
based on what produced a working mechanism within a 30-epoch budget.
They were not derived from first principles or found by an independent
search. This is the same kind of tuning that produced the fixed-alpha
values in the comparison, and the comparison should be weighed with that
in mind.

\textbf{The post-processing finding is conditional and not fully
characterized.} Two datasets are enough to show that post-processing's
win on COMPAS does not generalize unconditionally, and that
calibration-set size is the likely cause. They are not enough to find
the data size at which post-processing stops being reliable, or to rule
out other factors such as class balance, group-size imbalance or the
dimension of the feature space. This remains an open question.

\section{Conclusion}

Adaptive Alpha Control removes the need to hand-sweep a fixed weight in
adversarial debiasing, reaching a comparable operating point
automatically through dual ascent on two explicit fairness constraints.
The larger finding is that a much simpler post-hoc calibration method
outperforms it on COMPAS, though only when there is enough calibration
data. The choice between in-processing and post-processing debiasing
should depend on how much held-out data is available, and a
single-dataset benchmark in either direction is not enough to decide it.
Future work could locate this threshold more precisely, test whether a
hybrid (post-processing when data allows, adversarial training as a
fallback when it does not) outperforms either alone, and extend the
two-constraint dual-ascent idea to non-binary or intersectional
sensitive attributes.
