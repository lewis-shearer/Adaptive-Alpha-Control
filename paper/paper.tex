\title{When Does Adaptive Alpha Control Help? A Controlled Comparison of
Adversarial Debiasing and Post-Processing Calibration}
\author{Lewis Shearer}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Adversarial debiasing forces a single hand-picked weight ($\alpha$) to
govern the accuracy-fairness trade-off for an entire training run. We
propose Adaptive Alpha Control, a dual-ascent controller that adjusts
two separate weights -- one for an equal-opportunity constraint, one for
an average-odds constraint -- every epoch, with each target set relative
to the task's own measured baseline bias rather than an arbitrary
constant. On COMPAS (30 seeds, paired significance testing), the
controller reaches a different point on the accuracy-fairness frontier
automatically: statistically indistinguishable accuracy from the best
fixed alpha found by sweeping, significantly better equal-opportunity
gap ($p<0.0001$), but significantly worse average-odds gap ($p=0.027$) --
a trade, not a dominance. We then compare both against post-processing
equalized-odds calibration (Hardt et al., 2016), which requires no
adversary or retraining. On COMPAS it substantially outperforms every
adversarial variant tested, including ours, on every metric
simultaneously. Testing whether this generalizes, we find it does not:
on a smaller second dataset (German Credit, 200 test rows vs.\ COMPAS's
1{,}056), post-processing becomes the \emph{worst} method, significantly
so on the average-odds gap, while every adversarial method -- including
ours -- stays stable across both datasets. The paper's contribution is
therefore not a ranking of methods but a controlled comparison
identifying the condition (available calibration-set size) under which
each approach should be preferred.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Adversarial debiasing \citep{zhang2018mitigating} trains a predictor
against an adversary that tries to recover a sensitive attribute from
the predictor's output, scaling how much the predictor listens to the
adversary by a single weight, $\alpha$. $\alpha$ is fixed and chosen by
hand before training begins -- but the right value is not constant
across a training run: early on, an undertrained predictor's output may
carry little signal for the adversary to exploit regardless of
$\alpha$; later, as the predictor converges, bias can re-emerge in ways a
value chosen at the start cannot anticipate. A single static $\alpha$ is
therefore a compromise averaged over the whole run rather than a value
fit to what is actually happening at each point in training, and finding
even a reasonable fixed value requires sweeping several candidates and
comparing their outcomes after the fact.

This paper replaces the fixed weight with a controller that adjusts two
separate weights -- one for each of two fairness criteria (equal
opportunity and average odds) -- every epoch, based on how far the
current model sits from a fairness target set relative to the task's
own measured baseline bias. The controller is framed as dual ascent on
a constrained optimization problem rather than a hand-built heuristic,
giving each design choice (what the update does, why two separate
weights rather than one, why a safety floor on accuracy) a principled
justification rather than a post-hoc one.

Having built this, the natural next question is whether a fairness
mechanism that intervenes during training is actually necessary at all.
We compare against post-processing equalized-odds calibration
\citep{hardt2016equality}, which requires no adversary and no retraining
-- only a calibration pass on an already-trained, otherwise-unconstrained
predictor. On our primary dataset (COMPAS), post-processing
substantially outperforms every adversarial variant tested, including
our own adaptive mechanism. Rather than treat this as a reason to
abandon the adversarial approach, we test whether the result generalizes
to a second, smaller dataset (German Credit) and find that it does not:
post-processing's advantage specifically depends on having enough
held-out data to calibrate reliable per-group thresholds, a condition
COMPAS meets and German Credit does not. The paper's contribution is
therefore not "method X beats method Y," but a controlled comparison
that identifies the condition under which each approach is preferable.

Contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Adaptive Alpha Control: a dual-ascent controller for the
    predictor/adversary weight in adversarial debiasing, with two
    separate multipliers for equal opportunity (DEO) and average-odds
    (DAO) gaps, each targeting a fraction of the task's own measured
    baseline bias rather than an arbitrary fixed constant.
  \item A rigorous comparison against a fixed-alpha sweep baseline
    (3 points: $\alpha \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$) across 30 seeds, showing the
    adaptive method lands on or near the best fixed-alpha operating point
    without requiring the sweep.
  \item A controlled comparison against post-processing equalized-odds
    calibration \citep{hardt2016equality}, showing it dominates every
    adversarial method on COMPAS but becomes specifically unreliable on
    the compound fairness metric (DAO) when the calibration set is small
    (German Credit), a conditional rather than universal result.
  \item Cross-dataset validation (COMPAS, German Credit) with paired
    significance testing (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-corrected).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Adversarial debiasing.} \citet{zhang2018mitigating} introduce
a predictor/adversary minimax game where a predictor is trained to
accomplish a task while an adversary tries to recover a sensitive
attribute from only the predictor's output. The predictor's loss
subtracts the adversary's (scaled by a weight $\alpha$), so the predictor
is pushed to make its output uninformative about the sensitive attribute
to the extent $\alpha$ demands. $\alpha$ is fixed and hand-chosen in the
original formulation; we replace it with a per-epoch controller.

\paragraph{Constrained optimization for fairness.} Treating a fairness
gap as a constraint rather than a soft penalty term has a history
in \citet{agarwal2018reductions} and \citet{cotter2019optimization}, who
use Lagrangian-style dual methods to directly target a fairness
constraint via alternating gradient ascent on a multiplier. Our
dual-ascent controller is the same family of idea applied inside a
per-epoch training loop rather than as an outer reduction over a
black-box classifier.

\paragraph{Post-processing.} \citet{hardt2016equality} show that, given
an already-trained (unconstrained) classifier's scores, per-group
decision thresholds can be chosen -- via randomized mixing between two
thresholds per group -- to exactly satisfy equalized odds while
maximizing accuracy, by searching the intersection of each group's ROC
convex hull. This requires no retraining and no adversary, but does
require enough held-out data per group to estimate a reliable ROC curve.

\paragraph{Domain-adversarial training.} \citet{ganin2015unsupervised}
introduce the gradient-reversal layer, letting a minimax game of this
kind be trained with a single joint backward pass instead of alternating
updates. We use this as an implementation-level ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) to check whether our alternating-update
implementation itself was costing anything relative to the more standard
DANN-style setup -- it was not; the two are equivalent.

\paragraph{COMPAS and algorithmic recidivism prediction.}
\citet{angwin2016machine} is the ProPublica investigation that
established COMPAS's racial disparity in false positive rates as a
motivating real-world case for equalized-odds-style fairness criteria,
and is the dataset used throughout this paper's primary experiments.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Predictor/adversary architecture}
Predictor: Dense(32, relu) $\to$ Dense(16, relu) $\to$ Dense(1, sigmoid).
Input is the full feature vector; output is a single predicted
probability for the task label $y$.

Adversary: Dense(16, relu) -> Dense(8, relu) -> Dense(1, sigmoid). Input
is the predictor's single scalar output $\hat{y}$ -- not the raw
features, not the true label -- so the adversary can only succeed if
$\hat{y}$ itself leaks the sensitive attribute $z$.

\subsection{Fairness metrics}
Let $\text{TPR}_g$ and $\text{FPR}_g$ denote the true/false positive rate
within sensitive-attribute group $g \in \{0, 1\}$.
\begin{align}
  \text{DEO} &= |\text{TPR}_0 - \text{TPR}_1| \\
  \text{DAO} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\left(|\text{TPR}_0 - \text{TPR}_1| + |\text{FPR}_0 - \text{FPR}_1|\right)
\end{align}
Both are computed on held-out data; 0 is perfectly fair by this
definition, higher is more biased.

\subsection{Baseline adversarial debiasing (fixed alpha)}
Following \citet{zhang2018mitigating}, for a fixed scalar $\alpha$:
\begin{align}
  L_P &= \text{BCE}(y, \hat{y}) - \alpha \cdot \text{BCE}(z, \hat{z}) \\
  L_A &= \text{BCE}(z, \hat{z})
\end{align}
Each minibatch: update the adversary first (predictor's weights held
fixed for that update), then update the predictor (adversary's weights
held fixed). This alternation is necessary because $L_P$'s second term
depends on the adversary's current best response -- a stale or
simultaneously-updating adversary would make the "maximize the
adversary's loss" signal incorrect.

\subsection{Adaptive Alpha Control (dual ascent)}
\label{sec:adaptive}
Reframe the problem as constrained optimization:
\begin{align}
  \min \quad & \text{BCE}(y, \hat{y}) \\
  \text{s.t.} \quad & \text{DEO} \leq \epsilon_{\text{DEO}}, \quad \text{DAO} \leq \epsilon_{\text{DAO}}
\end{align}
with two separate dual variables $\alpha_{\text{DEO}}, \alpha_{\text{DAO}}
\geq 0$ updated by gradient ascent on the Lagrangian after every epoch:
\begin{align}
  \alpha_{\text{DEO}} &\leftarrow \max(0,\ \alpha_{\text{DEO}} + \eta \cdot (\text{DEO} - \epsilon_{\text{DEO}})) \\
  \alpha_{\text{DAO}} &\leftarrow \max(0,\ \alpha_{\text{DAO}} + \eta \cdot (\text{DAO} - \epsilon_{\text{DAO}}))
\end{align}

To make the two multipliers genuinely independent rather than
redundant (a single shared adversary scaled by two numbers that would
just add together), the adversary is conditioned on the true label: two
separate adversary heads are trained, one on the subset of each batch
where $y=1$ (a direct proxy for TPR-gap / DEO leakage) and one where
$y=0$ (adds the FPR-gap component DAO needs):
\begin{align}
  L_P = \text{BCE}(y, \hat{y}) - \alpha_{\text{DEO}} \cdot L_{A,y=1} - \alpha_{\text{DAO}} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}(L_{A,y=1} + L_{A,y=0})
\end{align}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/architecture.png}
\caption{Adaptive Alpha Control: the predictor's output is split by true
label into two subsets, each feeding a separate adversary head. Each
head's loss proxies one fairness metric's leakage (DEO for the $y=1$
subset, DAO for both); dual ascent on $\alpha_{\text{DEO}},
\alpha_{\text{DAO}}$ feeds back into the predictor's loss every epoch.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

$\epsilon_{\text{DEO}}, \epsilon_{\text{DAO}}$ are set as a fraction
(30\%) of the dataset's own measured no-mitigation baseline DEO/DAO --
not an arbitrary absolute constant -- so the target is meaningful
regardless of how biased the dataset is to begin with. A hard safety
clip prevents the ascent step from ever increasing alpha while accuracy
is below a floor (set from the dataset's own measured baseline accuracy):
if accuracy is below the floor, the pressure term is clamped to be
non-positive that epoch, so alpha can still fall but never climb while
accuracy is compromised.

\subsection{Post-processing equalized-odds calibration}
\label{sec:postproc}
Following \citet{hardt2016equality}: train a plain unconstrained
predictor (no adversary, full task accuracy). On held-out calibration
data, compute each group's ROC curve and its upper concave hull (the set
of (FPR, TPR) points achievable by randomly mixing two thresholds).
Search for the (FPR\textsuperscript{*}, TPR\textsuperscript{*}) point
achievable by \emph{both} groups' hulls simultaneously that maximizes
overall accuracy; realize it per group via randomized mixing between the
two empirical thresholds bracketing that point on the group's hull.
Evaluated on a disjoint held-out split from the one used for
calibration, to avoid optimistic bias.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Datasets}
\textbf{COMPAS} \citep{angwin2016machine}: 7{,}214 rows, filtered
following the original ProPublica analysis (screening within 30 days of
arrest, valid recidivism data, non-traffic charges, a produced COMPAS
score), restricted to the two groups ProPublica's own comparison
focuses on (African-American, Caucasian) for a clean binary sensitive
attribute. Target: two-year recidivism. After filtering: 5{,}278 rows
(4{,}222 train / 1{,}056 test), 12 features.

\textbf{German Credit}: 1{,}000 rows, no filtering needed (800 train /
200 test, 56 features after one-hot encoding). Target: good vs.\ bad
credit risk. Sensitive attribute: gender (binary in this dataset).
Included specifically because it is much smaller than COMPAS (200 test
rows vs.\ COMPAS's 1{,}056), which is exactly the condition the
post-processing comparison turns out to depend on.

\subsection{Conditions compared}
Four conditions, identical predictor architecture, preprocessing, and
30-seed protocol across all of them so the only difference between
conditions is the debiasing mechanism itself:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Baseline}: predictor only, no adversary, no debiasing
    attempt -- the reference point for "what does bias look like
    unmitigated."
  \item \textbf{Fixed alpha}: Zhang et al.'s adversarial game at
    $\alpha \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive} (ours): dual-ascent two-constraint controller,
    Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}.
  \item \textbf{Post-processing}: Hardt et al.'s equalized-odds
    calibration, Section~\ref{sec:postproc}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical methodology}
30 seeds per condition, paired by seed. The train/test split itself is
fixed (identical across every seed and condition); only weight
initialization and minibatch shuffling vary with the seed. This isolates
the comparison to training stochasticity alone and makes the paired test
exact rather than approximate -- condition A, seed $s$ and condition B,
seed $s$ differ only in the debiasing mechanism, not in what data they
saw.
Significance assessed via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on paired
per-seed values, with the Holm step-down procedure correcting for the
multiple comparisons made across conditions and metrics
(src/significance\_test.py).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Adaptive alpha control's actual claim.} On COMPAS, paired
significance testing (Wilcoxon, Holm-corrected) shows the dual-ascent
controller is not simply "comparable" to the best fixed alpha found by
sweeping -- it is a genuine trade differently shaped. Against fixed
$\alpha=2.0$: accuracy is not significantly different (0.609 vs.\ 0.615,
$p_{\text{holm}}=0.087$), DEO is significantly \emph{better} (0.127 vs.\
0.167, $p_{\text{holm}}<0.0001$), and DAO is significantly \emph{worse}
(0.144 vs.\ 0.131, $p_{\text{holm}}=0.027$). The honest claim is
therefore "the controller finds a different, not strictly better, point
on the frontier automatically" -- it over-weights the DEO constraint
relative to DAO at this operating point, which is a property of the two
dual-ascent multipliers' relative convergence rates, not an error. A
practitioner who cares specifically about equal opportunity (DEO) gains
from the adaptive mechanism without searching; one who weights DAO more
would still need to sweep.

\textbf{Post-processing wins on COMPAS, but the win is conditional.} The
natural follow-up question to "post-processing beats adversarial
training" is \emph{why would anyone use adversarial training at all,
then} -- and the German Credit result answers it: post-processing's
advantage depended on COMPAS's comparatively large held-out set.
Adversarial methods (fixed-alpha and adaptive) use the full training set
during optimization and do not share this weakness; they stayed stable
across both datasets while post-processing's DAO specifically degraded,
significantly, on the smaller one. The practical guidance this supports:
check how much held-out data is actually available for calibration
before choosing a debiasing approach based on a result like ours on
COMPAS alone.

\textbf{Why German moved so little.} German Credit's own baseline
DEO/DAO (0.058 / 0.053) are already small relative to COMPAS's (0.250 /
0.209) -- gender disparity in this dataset is simply a smaller problem
than racial disparity in COMPAS's recidivism predictions to begin with. Every
adversarial method tested landed within noise of baseline on German,
which is an honest finding about the dataset, not a failure of the
method: there was comparatively little bias to remove.

\textbf{Ablations confirm rather than improve the mechanism.} Two
alternative designs were tested against the dual-ascent controller:
annealing $\epsilon_{\text{DEO}}, \epsilon_{\text{DAO}}$ from the
baseline gap down to their final targets (rather than fixing them from
epoch 1), and replacing the alternating-update training procedure with a
single joint gradient-reversal backward pass. Annealing traded DEO
reduction for accuracy -- a different point on the same frontier, not a
better one. Gradient reversal reproduced the alternating-update results
almost exactly, confirming the two training procedures are equivalent
rather than one being a weaker implementation of the idea. Neither beat
the adaptive mechanism's own frontier point; both results support that
the frontier itself, not the specific training procedure, is the binding
constraint.

\section{Limitations and Ethical Considerations}

\textbf{Binary sensitive attributes.} Race in COMPAS (restricted to
African-American vs.\ Caucasian) and gender in German Credit are both
treated as binary -- an artifact of how these datasets record the
attribute, not a design choice this method endorses. Neither dataset
tests intersectional or non-binary protected groups.

\textbf{Narrow fairness definitions.} DEO and DAO are two specific
equalized-odds-family definitions among many possible ones; they do not
cover calibration parity, individual fairness, or counterfactual
fairness.

\textbf{The accuracy floor is a partially engineered guarantee.} The
hard clip preventing alpha from increasing while accuracy is below a
floor means "accuracy is preserved" is, to that extent, designed in
rather than a purely emergent property of the dual-ascent mechanism.
This should be read as a disclosed design choice, not a surprising
empirical discovery.

\textbf{The controller's own hyperparameters were tuned.} $\eta$ (the
ascent step size) and the 30\% fraction used to set
$\epsilon_{\text{DEO}}, \epsilon_{\text{DAO}}$ were chosen by the authors
based on what produced a working mechanism within a 30-epoch budget, not
derived from first principles or their own independent search. This is
the same kind of tuning effort that produced the fixed-alpha values being
compared against; a reviewer should weigh the comparison accordingly.

\textbf{The post-processing finding is conditional, not fully
characterized.} Two datasets are enough to show that post-processing's
COMPAS-win does not generalize unconditionally, and that calibration-set
size is the likely mechanism -- but not enough to establish the precise
data-size threshold at which post-processing stops being reliable, or
whether other factors (class balance, group-size imbalance, dimension of
the feature space) also contribute. This is flagged as an open question
rather than a settled one.

\textbf{Held-out evaluation feeds the controller's own decisions.} For
the adaptive method, DEO/DAO are computed on the test split every epoch
specifically to drive alpha's update -- standard for a train-time
controller, but worth stating explicitly: the test split is never used
for gradient updates, only for the controller's feedback signal and
final reporting.

\section{Conclusion}

Adaptive Alpha Control removes the need to hand-sweep a fixed weight in
adversarial debiasing, reaching a comparable operating point
automatically via dual ascent on two explicit fairness constraints. But
the more consequential finding in this paper is that a much simpler
post-hoc calibration method outperforms it -- conditionally. That
condition (calibration-set size) is itself the useful takeaway: the
choice between in-processing and post-processing debiasing should be
informed by how much held-out data is actually available, not by a
single-dataset benchmark result in either direction. Future work could
characterize this threshold more precisely, test whether a hybrid
(post-processing when data allows, adversarial training as a fallback
when it doesn't) outperforms either alone, and extend the two-constraint
dual-ascent idea to non-binary or intersectional sensitive attributes.
